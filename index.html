What the four have in common

Each company made a product that buyers wanted to try. In at least some of the cases, the cost of a factory came before sales could carry it. The public record does not always show whether the biology was ready for scale. The assets then flowed to whoever already owned a plant, as with Naplasol, or the company stopped growing fungi and kept the part that was hard to copy, as with MycoWorks.

This matches what First Bight Ventures finds across industrial biotech more broadly.

"Over 80% of industrial biotech failures aren't caused by bad science; they're caused by preventable execution breakdowns."First Bight Ventures, Founder's Guide

First Bight's Graveyard Report reviews thirty earlier failures. More than 80% of the companies made biofuels or platform chemicals, and more than 80% of the failures were not caused by the technology. The causes, in order, were market competition and product fit, scale up, and plant cost overruns, with first plants running three to five times over budget, as InnovATEBIO and Better Bioeconomy summarize the report. It advises starting with a niche, higher value product rather than a commodity.

The fungal wave mostly took that advice. Meati, MycoWorks, and Mylo aimed at premium consumer products, not fuel. Meati and MycoWorks still failed on the other lesson: they built their own plants before sales could pay for them. Picking the right market was not enough without renting the steel.

Public companies leave a record when they fail. Zymergen's includes its SEC filings, a 2024 SEC order with a $30 million penalty for overstating the market for its film, and a proposed $125 million shareholder settlement in 2026. Private companies, which is most of this map, leave a press release.

Lessons for the next fungal company

  • Rent the steel firstMake product in someone else's plant until sales justify your own. Mycorena's product lives on because an existing plant took it in.
  • Read the debt terms as closely as the scienceMeati's collapse began with a lender's targets, not a lab result, and it ended with a county tax warrant.
  • Know which step is truly yoursIf the raw material is a commodity, own the step that adds value, as MycoWorks now does with finishing.
  • Plan the wind down before you need itBolt Threads paused Mylo in 2023 and has announced no plan for the process since. Records that stay private inside a buyer or a paused company are lost to the field.

Who is building stewardship models

The Engine, the nonprofit incubator founded by MIT, announced its Climate Decelerator in September 2026 with support from the Schmidt Family Foundation. It offers short fellowships to founders whose companies are winding down or pivoting, and openly publishes what they learned about the technology and about scaling.

When SCiFi Foods closed in 2024, the Good Food Institute bought its cell lines and growth media at auction and banked three lines at the Tufts University Center for Cellular Agriculture, where any researcher can request them. In 2026 New Harvest opened a Knowledge Stewardship Initiative, which it describes as a service for cellular agriculture companies that are winding down and want their data rescued, recovered, and reused. The first agreement was signed in July 2026 by a cofounder of Meatable, a Dutch cultivated meat company that closed in 2025.

I found nothing like this for fungi, and the cultivated meat programs are early experiments rather than a finished model. The fungal field is small, the failures are recent, and many founders and staff are still reachable.

What the field cannot measure yet

Fungal materials and foods are sold by the ton, and there is still no agreed way to measure most of what decides whether they work. A reference material is a sample with a known value, sold so that any laboratory can check its own method against it. Fungi have none. This table is my assessment.

MeasureWhy it mattersWhat exists nowWhat is missing
Hyphal densitySets strength, feel, and consistency in a grown material.Each company built its own method. Phil Ross writes that there were no standard measures for hyphal density, tensile behavior, or substrate response, and that the infrastructure other industries built over generations had to be assembled from scratch, in real time, under commercial pressure.An agreed method, and a sample of known value to check it against.
Tensile and compressive behaviorDecides whether a grown panel can replace a conventional one.Published studies use test methods written for conventional composites and apply them case by case. I found no standard test method written for mycelium materials.Test methods written for grown materials, and one comparison run across several laboratories.
Substrate responseYield and material properties change with the substrate, which is usually a waste stream and never uniform.In house recipes, rarely published. Some groups borrow a mass produced food product because its composition is uniform, which shows the size of the gap rather than closing it.A reference substrate that any laboratory can buy.
Contamination control at volumeDecides whether a plant can finish a full campaign.Company protocols, unpublished.Shared protocols and an agreed limit.
Strain driftYield falls as a strain changes over generations, and nobody outside a company can see it happen.Internal models at a few companies, now at risk as those companies close.A documented way to detect drift, and archived reference strains.
Yield per unit substrateThe single number a grower or an investor needs.Missing from nearly all published submerged fermentation studies, in a 2025 review of 216 treatments by GFI Israel.Reporting rules that make the number mandatory.

How other fields closed this gap

The United States National Institute of Standards and Technology supplies a reference material made of living yeast, RM 8230, so that laboratories can compare their measurements of microbial cells. It supplies a monoclonal antibody, NISTmAb, whose use more than 100 collaborators from companies, regulators and universities have tested, so that the industry can check its own quality control. In 2025 it released NISTCHO, a living cell line that makes that antibody. In a 2025 study of candidate reference materials for lentiviral vector copy number, the same blinded samples went to 12 laboratories, and all 12 identified the right values. A certified cell is an ordinary thing.

The reason to bother is visible in a neighboring field. In cell free biology, a 2022 study by Rhea and colleagues found that two laboratories following the same written protocol saw yields differ by as much as eightfold, with causes that ran from the operator and the reagents down to the instruments, the plates, and the way a signal was converted into standard units. Sharing a protocol is not enough. Calibration against a common material is what makes results comparable. NIST held workshops on reproducibility in cell free systems with the J. Craig Venter Institute in 2019 and with the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences in 2024, the second on scale up and automation.

Synthetic cell research shows the other half of the answer. b.next publishes Nucleus, an open specification for the materials needed to build cell free synthetic cells, launched in December 2023. Its team found that only a handful of laboratories had ever built the underlying system, first published in 2001, and that the supply of materials was fragile. In 2026 it built a small plant of 1,100 square feet to make those materials itself.

In fungi the only measurement body visibly at work is AOAC, whose Functional Mushrooms Initiative was announced in 2025, starting with lion's mane under a botanical integrity program. That work addresses whether a supplement contains what its label says. It does not touch materials, growth, or production.

This section records a gap, not a program. A national measurement institute, a standards body, a university, or a nonprofit could each close it. None has claimed it.
